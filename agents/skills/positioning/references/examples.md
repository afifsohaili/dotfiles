# Positioning examples

Source: FletchPMM's positioning model, as taught by Anthony Pierri in "How to Perfectly Position Your B2B Brand" (MicroConf, 2025), plus before/after teardowns published at fletchpmm.com.

## Loom, end to end

Loom is a screen recorder. The position is not "screen recording for everyone."

| Box | Fill |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow | Providing regular updates to coworkers |
| Competitive alternative | A meeting |
| Aware problem | Meetings are long, boring, and waste time. The buyer already thinks "this could have been an email." |
| Category | Screen recorder (a mature, unsexy shelf) |
| Differentiation | Binary: record screen and face at once, send a link, no meeting. Not another screen recorder. |

Positioning statement:

> For people who send regular updates to coworkers, who lose hours to meetings, Loom is a screen recorder that captures screen and face at once. Unlike a meeting, it sends the update as a link, so the meeting never happens.

Hero: "Loom on. Meetings off." / "Record quick videos to update your team and cut down meetings by 29%."

Problem section: "Meetings are unproductive and waste everyone's time. You chitchat. Nobody preps. You re-explain the context. Most 30-minute meetings could have been five."

Solution intro: "The easiest screen recorder you'll ever use. Record in a few clicks, share anywhere, collaborate better."

Product highlights:

| Feature | Capability | Benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chrome extension, desktop and mobile apps | Record screen and camera at once | Send the update without booking a meeting |
| Automatic transcription | Highlight and delete text to edit video | Edit video as easily as a document |
| Unique share link | View without an account | Send to anyone, including clients |
| Viewer analytics | See who watched and how far | Know who needs a follow-up |

Why it worked: Loom did not invent a category. It put an existing category in front of a group that did not shop for it, against a behavior everyone already resented.

## Workflow level sets the business

| Company | Workflow | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calendly | Schedule a meeting | Low price, self-serve, appears in sales, teaching, counseling, healthcare |
| Gainsight | End-to-end customer success | High price, long cycle, multiple departments, category ownership |

A high-level workflow ("we own revenue") invites buyers to ask for pieces you do not do. A low-level workflow can look small and still travel across industries.

## Problem location

Cold email software, two valid positions:

| Location | Position | What the page argues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In the workflow | Cold email is broken. Do warm intros. | Replace the behavior. The product enables a different workflow. |
| In the alternative | Cold email works. Other tools have bad deliverability. | Replace the tool. Keep the behavior; remove its defect. |

Lemlist took the second: the problem lives in other sales-engagement tools, not in outbound itself.

## Differentiation type

| Type | Test | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Degree | Is it 10x, not 10%? | Superhuman is faster than Gmail because of shortcuts and snippets. |
| Binary | Can the alternative copy it without changing its business? | DuckDuckGo never tracks you. Google's model cannot say that. |

Below 10x, a degree claim becomes a feature. A binary claim is stronger only when the alternative truly cannot follow.

## Category choice

Servikit collected customer feedback. Two shelves:

| Shelf | Pitch | Trade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survey tool (mature) | "A survey tool that also listens on review sites and calls." | Buyers understand it. You must beat survey incumbents. |
| Customer feedback platform (emerging) | "Surveys are one small corner. Get feedback everywhere." | No education ceiling, but no one is shopping for it yet. |

Product-led sales showed the downside of the emerging bet: a funded category with a label but no durable buyer behavior.

Miro is the other failure: it calls itself an "innovation workspace," while buyers search for a "digital whiteboard." The internal label sounds bigger; the buyer's label gets found. Use the shelf buyers already search, then stretch it.

## FletchPMM before/after deltas

### Administrate

Before:

- Broad, vague.
- Unclear exactly what problem or problems it solves.
- Differentiation takes a back seat to business outcomes.

After:

- Focused on the key use case.
- Highlights the main problems and demonstrates how Administrate solves them.
- Differentiation takes center stage, which makes the business outcomes more believable.

### Lemlist

Before:

- Broad, vague positioning.
- No mention of the problems the platform solves.
- Feature-heavy.

After:

- Positioned on deliverability, the key differentiator.
- Highlights key use cases.
- Details the problem being solved.

### Superhuman

Before:

- Broad framing of the problem sales teams face with email.
- Over-messaging on benefits, missing clear product differentiation.
- Feature overload.
- Extremely long page, unlikely to get read.

After:

- Spotlights specific pains account executives face with email.
- Leads with sharper capabilities, supporting sales-level benefits.
- Prioritizes the capabilities a sales leader would care about.
- More concise narrative structure.

### UserEvidence

Before:

- Leads with a vague outcome.
- Broader problem statement.
- Leaves people wondering "but what does the product actually do?"
- Department-agnostic message.

After:

- Leads with the most important use case: collecting customer stories.
- Highlights a sharper pain point: asking customers one by one.
- Demonstrates exactly how the product solves the problem.
- Leads with the most important department: marketing.

### Zocks

Before:

- Horizontal: aimed at any "privacy-sensitive industry" with no named customer.
- Leads with a vague outcome ("capture data that matters") instead of saying what the product is.
- Mixes audiences on one page: end users, developers, and API buyers.

After:

- Names the category and the customer: the meeting assistant for financial advisors.
- Problem statement is specific to the advisor's day: note taking, missed financial details, manual CRM entry.
- Leads with concrete capabilities: financial fact capture, follow-up emails, CRM and task manager integrations.

## The copy teardown

Every after page does the same five things: narrows to one buyer and one workflow, names the category, states the problem as a scene from that buyer's day, puts differentiation above the outcome, and shows the mechanism instead of listing features. Every before page fails at least two.

The recurring moves:

### Headline formulas

| Formula | Shipped example | Company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The [category] made for [buyer] | "The Meeting Assistant made for Financial Advisors" | Zocks |
| The only [category] that [differentiator] | "The only cold outreach tool that helps you reach inboxes and get replies" | Lemlist |
| Get the [category] that [outcome] | "Get the email app that keeps deals moving" | Superhuman |
| The [category] [buyer] graduate to | "The subscription platform top Shopify brands graduate to" | Skio |
| Build your [category] at [speed] | "Build your Enterprise CRM at AI Speed" | Twenty |
| Don't [painful behavior]. [New outcome]. | "Don't beg for case studies. Get customer proof at scale." | UserEvidence |
| [Old belief] is dead. [Object] is the problem. | "Outbound isn't dead. Your lead list is." | Tamtam |
| Everything you need to [job] | "Everything You Need To Manage Live Customer Training" | Administrate |

Before headlines failed the same way: metaphor, superlative, or generic negative. "Capture data that matters." "Turn Strangers into Customers." "Revolutionary AI email." Only Twenty named a category; none named a buyer and a workflow.

### The page

- **Subhead = mechanism.** Three workflow verbs plus the outcome. "Build your lead list, write and personalize at scale, and send cold emails that actually get customers."
- **Problem block at section three.** Three pains, each mapping one-to-one to the next three capabilities. Headings are full sentences: "Deals die in your reps' cluttered inboxes."
- **Features become a numbered workflow.** Three or four steps, verb-first: list → write → deliver; plan → schedule → communicate → handle issues; surface → research → find → recommend.
- **Proof moves into the first two screens.** A buyer-specific logo strip, a three-number band, and one named human with a role and a number. Testimonial walls become one hero quote.
- **CTA discipline.** One primary verb ("Sign up", "Get a demo", "Launch Run") and one human-help secondary ("Talk to Sales", "Contact us"). Repeat them. No waitlist survives.
- **Section order.** Hero → logos → problem → bridge → 3–4 steps with product UI → proof → use cases, integrations, security → pricing, FAQ → final CTA restating the H1.
- **Cut inventory for second audiences.** API and SDK buyers, partners, community, enterprise, extra modules. Keep the logo wall, product UI, pricing, and FAQ.
- **Length is not the tell.** Superhuman shrank 32%, Lemlist 16%, Zocks 13%, Tamtam 18%. Skio grew 37%, UserEvidence 19%, Administrate 8%, Twenty 5%. The tell is the ratio of argument to inventory.

### Language

- "Your client," "your reps," "your CRM" over "our platform."
- "Without [friction]" and "so [outcome]" to connect mechanism to benefit.
- "Stop [behavior]" to name what the buyer should abandon.
- Colloquial villains beat brand language: "garbage list," "Franken-stack," "glorified stenographer," "10 year old tech."
- Specificity beats polish. "Modern subscriptions brands" survives on a live page. Do not sand off the meaning while fixing the grammar.

Not every winner follows every rule. Administrate names the job, not the category. UserEvidence names the pain. Tamtam names the object. The question is what this buyer buys, not which formula to obey.

## Before smells

Use these as a scan, not a scorecard:

- "Platform," "workspace," "orchestration," "AI-powered."
- An outcome headline with no product noun.
- A customer described by firmographics only.
- Three audiences on one page.
- Features listed without the alternative they beat.
- A page long enough that the hero could be removed and nothing would be lost.
