# Positioning

Positioning answers three questions: who is this for, what is it, and why is it better. Copy comes last. A page that answers all three in one scroll beats a prettier page that answers none.

Founders can explain the product for 30 minutes. A homepage gets 15 seconds. Positioning is the compression.

The order is load-bearing. Most bad pages start with copy and reverse-engineer a position. Do not do that.

New page: fill the five boxes, then write. Existing page: start with [Diagnose](#diagnose-an-existing-page), then fill the boxes.

## The five boxes

Fill these in order. Each box ends with a completion criterion; do not move on until it holds.

If you cannot fill a box from evidence, ask. Do not invent a customer, a complaint, a competitor, or a number.

| Box | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow | What recurring job does the buyer already do? |
| Competitive alternative | What do they use today if you do not exist? |
| Aware problem | What do they already complain about? |
| Category | What shelf do they search on? |
| Differentiation | Why switch? |

## 1. Workflow

All software is workflow software. The workflow is the market segment, not the firmographic.

A company that matches your industry, size, and revenue is not in your market unless it does the workflow your product supports. "Increase revenue" is an outcome, not a workflow. Nobody has "increase revenue" from 2 to 3 p.m.

Firmographics, industry, and department narrow the segment after the workflow. They cannot define it alone.

Workflow level is a business decision:

| Level | Price | Sales cycle | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High (own revenue, own customer success) | High | Long, multi-department | Scope confusion: buyers ask for parts you do not do |
| Low (schedule a meeting, record a screen) | Low | Short, self-serve | Low price, but the workflow appears in many industries |

A low-level workflow can travel across industries. Calendly built a large business on scheduling, a workflow that shows up in sales, teaching, counseling, and healthcare. That reach came from the workflow, not from a horizontal brand.

**Done when:** the workflow appears in the buyer's calendar or job description. If it only appears in your pitch, it is not a workflow.

## 2. Competitive alternative

The alternative is what the buyer does now. It is often not a product in your category.

- Slack replaced email, not Discord.
- Loom replaced a meeting, not other screen recorders.
- A spreadsheet, a VA, an intern, or "nothing" can be the alternative.

Ask: what does this replace? If you cannot name it, the value proposition has no anchor. The hero contrasts against it, and the problem section describes it.

**Done when:** you can name the alternative without naming a direct competitor.

## 3. Aware problem

The problem must already exist in the buyer's mind. You cannot position a problem into existence.

It lives in one of two places. Pick one:

| Location | Claim | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In the workflow | Stop doing this work. Do a different one. | "Cold email is broken. Do warm intros instead." |
| In the alternative | Keep the workflow. Our version removes its defect. | "Keep cold email. Ours lands in the inbox." |

This choice sets the page. Problem-in-workflow means the product replaces a behavior. Problem-in-alternative means the product replaces a tool.

**Done when:** you can quote the complaint in the buyer's words, with your product removed from the sentence.

## 4. Category

The category is a shelf in the buyer's mind, and usually a search query.

| Choice | Gain | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mature category | No education. Buyer knows what it is. | You fight incumbents on "why better." You need a 10x or binary answer. |
| Emerging category | Buzz, room to define, no price anchor. | You pay for education. The category may die. |

Name the shelf the buyer already uses, then stretch it. Do not invent a category with no demand and call it thought leadership. Product-led sales is the cautionary case: venture money, a promising label, no durable buyer.

**Done when:** a stranger can finish "Oh, it's a ___" with a noun you chose, and that noun is something buyers search for.

## 5. Differentiation

Two kinds, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Degree:** same job, 10x faster, cheaper, or better. Below 10x it is a feature, not a position.
- **Binary:** a thing you do that the alternative structurally cannot or will not do. DuckDuckGo never tracks you; Google's business model cannot make that claim.

Lead with the differentiation. Outcomes become believable after the mechanism is clear. A page that leads with outcomes and hides the mechanism reads like every competitor.

**Done when:** the line would be false on a competitor's site.

## Write the position

Use this shape:

> For [people doing workflow], who [aware problem], [product] is a [category] that [differentiation]. Unlike [alternative], it [capability], so [outcome].

Loom:

> For people who send regular updates to coworkers, who lose hours to meetings, Loom is a screen recorder that captures screen and face at once. Unlike a meeting, it sends the update as a link, so the meeting never happens.

One sentence is enough. If it needs a paragraph, the position is still fuzzy.

## The page

The homepage is the alignment tool. A position that lives only in a deck never becomes real. Put it on the page, where the team, buyers, and investors all read the same answer.

### Hero

The first scroll must answer all three questions: who, what, why better. Not just the outcome.

Patterns that do the work:

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contrast | [Product] on, [alternative] off | "Loom on. Meetings off." |
| Category + customer | The [category] for [workflow] | "The meeting assistant for financial advisors." |
| Category + binary | The [category] that [binary difference] | "The search engine that never tracks you." |
| Only | The only [category] that [differentiator] | "The only cold outreach tool that helps you reach inboxes and get replies." |
| Prohibition | Don't [behavior]. [New outcome]. | "Don't beg for case studies. Get customer proof at scale." |

Then one line with the mechanism and a real number if you have one. Loom: "Record quick videos to update your team and cut down meetings by 29%."

The headline is the claim. The subhead is the mechanism: three workflow verbs and the outcome.

### Problem

Give the problem its own block. Describe the workflow as it runs today, in the buyer's words, and name the cost. This is the setup that makes the solution legible.

Three pains, each mapping to one capability below. If a pain has no matching capability, cut it.

### Solution intro

Re-pitch the product in the language of the problem you just named. Do not introduce a new problem here.

### 15-second demo

Three or four product highlights. Each one follows feature → capability → benefit:

- **Feature:** what it is. "Chrome extension, desktop app, mobile app."
- **Capability:** what the buyer does with it. "Record screen and camera at the same time."
- **Benefit:** what changes. "Send the update without booking a meeting."

Order by what the buyer cares about, not by the product's navigation. Show the product.

**Done when:** a skimmer understands the mechanism in 15 to 30 seconds. Nail the hero, the problem and solution, and this demo, and the page is in the top 1% of website messaging.

### Then modular

Proof, integrations, objections, security, resources, pricing. These support the position; they cannot replace it. Length is not a substitute for clarity.

## Copy rules

- **Trace every line to a box.** If a line serves none of the five, cut it. If it serves a different box than the section needs, rewrite it.
- **Use specific nouns.** "Financial advisors," not "privacy-sensitive industries." "Meeting notes and CRM entry," not "capture data that matters."
- **One audience per page.** End users, developers, and API buyers on one page means no one is the ICP.
- **Differentiation before outcome.** The outcome is believed only after the mechanism is clear.
- **Headlines carry the argument.** Most visitors read headings only. Every heading must survive alone.
- **Shorter.** A long page is usually a symptom of an unclear position. Cut sections that repeat the same claim.
- **No category cosplay.** "Platform," "workspace," "orchestration," and "AI-powered" are usually empty. Keep them only if the buyer searches for them.
- **Do not let a sentence move the position.** If a line sounds good but changes who, what, or why, fix the position, not the sentence.

## Diagnose an existing page

Symptoms of a positioning problem, not a copy problem:

- People read the page and cannot repeat what you do.
- Buyers cannot say why you beat the alternative.
- You cannot choose between several audiences.
- You spend the sales call educating the market on what the product is.
- The product suite has become a Frankenstein of unrelated tools.

Read the page the way a buyer does: headings only, 15 seconds. Write down the sentence you would repeat to a colleague.

Then trace the failure to a box:

1. The sentence names no person and no workflow → box 1, Workflow.
2. The sentence names no category the buyer knows → box 4, Category.
3. The sentence names no differentiator the alternative cannot claim → box 5, Differentiation.
4. The page states an outcome but not the problem → box 3, Aware problem.
5. The page never names what it replaces → box 2, Competitive alternative.

Then check the page itself:

| Failure | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| "For everyone," horizontal | Pick one workflow. Name the person who does it. |
| Vague outcome ("capture data that matters") | Say what the product is and what it replaces. |
| Feature dump | Pair every feature with the alternative it beats. |
| Outcome-first, no mechanism | Lead with differentiation. |
| Invented category, no demand | Use the nearest real shelf, then stretch it. |
| Long page | Cut to hero, problem, solution, demo. Move the rest. |
| Mixed audiences | Split by workflow, not by persona. |
| A competitor's logo would fit unchanged | Box 5 is empty. Write a line that is false on their site. |

## Output

When asked to position or rewrite a page, return:

1. The five boxes, filled.
2. The positioning statement.
3. A wireframe: section, job, headline, one or two lines of body.
4. The copy, section by section.
5. A trace: each section to the box it serves.

Ask only for what you cannot infer: what the buyer does today, what they complain about, and who signs the check. Do not ask for a brand voice guide before the position is clear.
